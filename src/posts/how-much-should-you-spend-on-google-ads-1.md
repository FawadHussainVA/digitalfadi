Almost every first conversation includes this question, and almost every answer you will find online is useless. "Spend 5 to 10 percent of revenue." "Start with 50 dollars a day." "Match your competitors." None of these know anything about your margins, your close rate or what a customer is worth to you, which are the only three things that decide the answer.



The right budget is not a number somebody hands you. It is something you work out, and the arithmetic is simpler than it looks.



\## Start with what a customer is worth



Before anything else, work out two figures.



\*\*What you earn from one customer.\*\* Not the sale price, the gross profit. If you sell a 400 dollar service that costs you 150 to deliver, the customer is worth 250, not 400. If people buy again, use the profit across the whole relationship rather than the first order.



\*\*How many enquiries it takes to win one.\*\* If one in four quotes turns into a job, four enquiries produce one customer.



Those two numbers give you everything else. In this example a customer is worth 250 in profit, and it takes four enquiries to get one, so an enquiry is worth about 62 dollars.



\## Work backwards to a maximum cost per lead



You now know what you can pay for an enquiry before the channel stops making money. Sixty two dollars is break even. Nobody wants break even, so decide what margin you need. If you want to keep half, your target cost per enquiry is around 31 dollars.



That single number is the one to manage the account against. Not clicks, not impressions, not click-through rate. Every bid, every keyword and every campaign decision is judged against whether it moves that figure.



\## Why percentage-of-revenue rules do not work



A rule like "spend 10 percent of revenue" ignores margin completely. A business selling software at 90 percent margin and a business selling appliances at 12 percent margin cannot possibly run the same rule, because one can pay seven times more for the same customer.



Competitor benchmarks fail for the same reason. You cannot see their margins, their close rate, their repeat purchase rate or whether their account is even profitable. Copying a competitor's budget is copying somebody's homework without knowing if they passed.



\## The minimum that makes a test meaningful



There is a floor below which Google Ads cannot tell you anything useful, and it is set by your own cost per click, not by a fixed dollar amount.



To learn whether the channel works, you need enough conversions to trust the pattern. A rough rule is thirty conversions before you draw conclusions. So take your target cost per enquiry, multiply by thirty, and that is roughly what a first honest test costs. In the example above that is about 930 dollars, spread over a month.



If that number is uncomfortable, the answer is not to spend a third of it and hope. A budget too small to produce data buys you nothing except a bill. It is better to narrow the targeting hard, run in one city or on one service, and reach a real conclusion about a small area than to spread a thin budget across everything and learn nothing.



\## What underspending actually costs



Thin budgets fail in ways that are not obvious.



\- Campaigns stop showing partway through the day, so you only see part of the demand and usually not the best part.

\- Smart Bidding needs conversion data to work. Starve it and it cannot learn, so it defaults to expensive guesses.

\- Results swing wildly week to week, which tempts people into changing things constantly, which resets learning and makes results swing more.
