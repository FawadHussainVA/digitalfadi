Google Ads for dentists has no fixed price. You set your own budget, and what each click costs depends on your area, the treatment, the competition and the quality of your ads.

The useful way to plan is backwards: work out what a new patient is worth to the practice, decide what you can afford to pay to win one, and set a budget that can produce enough patients at that cost. Management fees, if you hire someone, come on top of the advertising itself.

This article explains what drives the cost and how to set a sensible budget. For the general method across all industries, see [how much should you spend on Google Ads?](/learn/how-much-should-you-spend-on-google-ads/)

## The two costs you pay

1. **Ad spend:** paid to Google for clicks, or for leads with Local Services Ads. This is the advertising itself
2. **Management:** paid to whoever runs the account, if you do not do it yourself

Keep them separate when you compare options. A low management fee with wasted ad spend can cost far more than a fair fee with a well-run account.

## What makes dental clicks cost more or less

- **Location.** Busy cities with many practices usually cost more per click than smaller towns
- **Treatment.** Implant and cosmetic searches are often more expensive than check-up searches, because the value per patient is higher and more practices compete
- **Competition.** The number of practices, groups and marketing companies bidding on the same searches
- **Ad and landing page quality.** Relevant ads and pages can lower what you pay for the same position. [What is Quality Score?](/learn/what-is-quality-score-in-google-ads/) explains how
- **Match types and negatives.** Loose targeting pays for searches that never become patients. See [negative keywords for dental practices](/learn/dental-negative-keywords/)
- **Time of day and device.** Emergency searches in the evening may cost differently from routine searches in the morning

Because every market is different, the only reliable cost figures are your own: the cost per click and cost per new patient from your account, or from a short, well-tracked test.

## Set the budget from the value of a patient

Work backwards in four steps:

1. **What is a new patient worth?** Use the average first-year value, or the value of the treatment you are advertising. Implant and cosmetic cases are worth much more than a check-up
2. **What can you pay to win one?** Decide the share of that value you are willing to spend on advertising, leaving a profit
3. **What conversion rate do you expect?** Of the people who click, how many book? If you have no data yet, measure it in the first weeks rather than guessing
4. **How many new patients do you want each month?**

For example, purely as an illustration: if you decide you can pay up to 150 for a new general patient, and you want 20 new patients a month, the account needs to be able to spend around 3,000 a month at that cost per patient. If your real cost per patient turns out lower, you either get more patients or spend less. Replace every number here with your own.

The [metrics calculator](/tools/metrics-calculator/) and [budget calculator](/tools/budget-calculator/) on this site do this maths for you.

## Split the budget by treatment

Not every pound or dollar should go to the same place:

- **Emergency** campaigns need enough budget to stay visible during the hours you can answer calls
- **General and new patient** campaigns are often the steadiest source of new patients
- **Implant and cosmetic** campaigns need a budget that allows for a longer decision and a higher cost per lead

Starting with one or two campaigns and expanding once tracking proves them is usually safer than spreading a small budget across everything.

## Local Services Ads: paying per lead

Where dentists can use Local Services Ads, you pay per qualifying lead rather than per click. That makes cost easier to predict, but compare it fairly: judge Local Services Ads and Search on cost per booked patient over the same period, not on the price of a lead against the price of a click.

## What management costs

How it is charged varies:

- **A fixed monthly fee,** which does not rise automatically when your budget grows
- **A percentage of ad spend,** common for larger accounts
- **Project fees** for audits, setup or rebuilds

For reference, my own prices are published: a one-time account analysis is $100, management is $499 a month, or 10% of managed ad spend for larger accounts. Your ad spend is always paid directly to Google from your own account. [Google Ads freelancer vs agency](/learn/google-ads-freelancer-vs-agency/) compares the options.

## Costs that do not show on the invoice

- **Missed calls** during the hours ads are running
- **Unbooked enquiries** that nobody followed up
- **Wasted clicks** from searches you never wanted
- **Wrong tracking,** which makes automated bidding spend on the wrong things

These often cost more than the difference between two management fees. [Dental Google Ads mistakes that waste your budget](/learn/dental-google-ads-mistakes/) covers the common ones.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should a dental practice spend on Google Ads per month?

Work backwards from the value of a new patient and the number of new patients you want. Divide what you can afford per patient by your conversion rate to see what you can afford per click, then set a budget that allows enough clicks for your target. There is no single right number for every practice.

### Why are dental implant clicks so expensive?

Because an accepted implant case is worth a lot, many practices compete for the same searches. A higher cost per click can still be profitable if enough consultations become accepted treatment.

### Is it cheaper to manage dental Google Ads myself?

It saves the management fee, but it takes regular time and knowledge. If the account wastes money on irrelevant searches or tracks the wrong conversions, the saving can disappear quickly.

### Do I pay Google or the person managing my ads?

Usually both, separately. Ad spend should be paid directly to Google from an account registered to your practice. The management fee goes to whoever runs it.

### What is a good cost per new patient for a dentist?

It depends on what a new patient is worth to your practice. A good cost per new patient is one that leaves a clear profit after the value of their treatment, so set your own target from your numbers.
