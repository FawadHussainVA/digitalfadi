ROAS, or return on ad spend, is the most quoted number in Google Ads and the least understood. A ROAS of 400% sounds excellent. Whether it is actually profitable depends on one thing the ad platform does not know: how much of each sale you keep.

## What break-even ROAS means

Break-even ROAS is the return at which your advertising exactly pays for itself. Below it, every sale loses you money once the cost of the product is taken out. Above it, you make a profit on the advertising.

The formula is simple:

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin

Gross margin is the share of each sale left after the cost of the goods, shipping and any payment fees. If your gross margin is 40%, your break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ 0.40, which is 2.5. In other words, each $1 of ad spend has to bring in at least $2.50 of revenue before you are even back to zero.

For example, suppose a product sells for $100 and keeps $40 after its direct costs. Spending $40 on ads to win one sale leaves you at zero. Any cheaper sale produces profit, and any more expensive sale produces a loss.

## Why a good-looking ROAS can still lose money

Take two accounts, both with a ROAS of 400%. The first sells products with a 60% margin, so its break-even is about 1.7 and it is comfortably profitable. The second sells products with a 20% margin, so its break-even is 5. At 400% it is losing money on every sale.

The same ROAS can be a win for one business and a slow loss for another. The platform reports revenue. It has no idea what your margin is, which is why the target has to come from your own numbers.

## Setting a target in Google Ads

Google's Smart Bidding strategies let you set a target ROAS. The most common mistake is to set it at break-even, which leaves no room for the platform to learn and no room for error. A sensible approach is to start a little above break-even, let the campaign gather data, and then lower the target gradually while watching the results.

Set the target by product group where your margins differ widely. A single target across a catalogue with a 15% margin on some lines and a 60% margin on others will chase the wrong sales.

Also check what the revenue figure includes. Returns, discounts and cancelled orders should be taken out before you judge the number, or the account will look better than it is.

## Checking it before you change anything

Before you move a target, confirm that conversion tracking records the sales you care about, with the right values. A broken revenue value makes every ROAS number meaningless. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is break-even ROAS?

Break-even ROAS is the return on ad spend at which your advertising covers its own cost. It equals 100% divided by your gross margin, so a 40% margin needs a ROAS of 250% just to break even. Any ROAS below that loses money once product costs are counted.

### Why can a 400% ROAS still lose money?

A 400% return only covers ad spend if your gross margin is above 25%. Below that margin, the revenue left after product costs is smaller than the advertising spend, so every sale loses money. Shipping, returns and fees lower the margin further, so count them before setting a target.
