The report says calls are up and cost per lead is down. The business says the phone rings with suppliers, recruiters and people who wanted somebody forty miles away.

Both are telling the truth. The gap between them is almost always the conversion definition, and once that is fixed the whole account changes.

## A timer is not a qualification

The default call conversion counts any call over a set duration, often as little as thirty or sixty seconds. Some accounts are set to fifteen.

Fifteen seconds is long enough for a wrong number. Sixty is long enough for a sales call from a software vendor.

So Smart Bidding, doing exactly what it was told, learns to buy calls rather than customers. It finds the searches that make phones ring cheaply, which are rarely the searches that make people book.

The fix is to count a booked job rather than a call, and feed that back to the account.

## Offline conversion imports

This is the mechanism and it is less work than it sounds.

When a call comes through, Google provides a click identifier. If that identifier is stored against the enquiry in your booking system or CRM, you can later upload which ones became jobs, with their value.

Bidding then optimises towards the keywords and times of day that produce work, not noise.

In practice:

1. Call tracking captures the click identifier with the call
2. The booking system records whether it became a job, and what it was worth
3. That outcome is uploaded back to Google Ads, weekly is usually enough
4. Bidding starts preferring the sources that produced jobs

The first month of accurate data usually shows cost per booked job is well above what was being reported. That is uncomfortable and it is the number the business can act on.

## Separate the conversion actions

Not every enquiry deserves the same weight.

A form fill, a call, and a booked job are three different events. Counting them as one conversion teaches the algorithm that a two minute wrong number equals a signed job.

Give each its own conversion action and its own value. The ratios matter more than the absolute numbers.

## Ad scheduling against who answers

Paying for calls when nobody picks up is the most avoidable waste in local accounts.

Check your call reporting for unanswered calls by hour and day. If adverts run until midnight and the office closes at six, you are buying calls that go to voicemail, and most callers simply ring the next result.

Either extend answering or stop advertising into hours nobody covers. For genuine emergency services the answer is the first, for everyone else the second.

## Geography by travel time, not radius

Most local accounts use one radius around one pin, which treats a job twenty minutes away the same as one an hour away.

Travel time decides whether a job is profitable. Split locations by how far crews will actually go, bid differently by area, and exclude the places where the drive eats the margin.

The location setting itself matters too. The default includes people merely interested in your area, which for a plumber means paying for somebody researching from another country. Set it to presence rather than presence or interest.

## Emergency and scheduled work are different businesses

Someone searching "emergency boiler repair" at eleven at night converts at a different rate, at a different price, than someone searching "boiler service" on a Tuesday afternoon.

Sharing one campaign means one bid strategy, one schedule and one set of adverts across two completely different buying situations. Splitting them lets each be priced and scheduled properly.

## What good looks like

- Conversions counted on booked jobs, not call duration
- Offline conversion imports running
- Separate actions and values for calls, forms and jobs
- Adverts scheduled against hours the phone is answered
- Locations split by travel time with the presence-only setting
- Emergency separated from planned work
