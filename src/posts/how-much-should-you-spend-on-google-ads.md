Spend enough to buy the conversions you need, at a cost per conversion the business can afford, and no less than it takes to learn something within a month or two. That is the whole answer. The useful part is turning it into a number.

Most budgets are set the other way round: somebody picks a figure that feels safe, and the account is then judged on whatever that figure happens to buy. A budget too small to produce a meaningful number of enquiries does not save money. It spends money without telling you anything.

## The short version: the budget formula

Monthly budget = conversions you need &times; what you can afford to pay for each one.

Everything below is about filling in those two numbers honestly.

## Step 1: work out what a conversion is worth

Start with profit, not revenue. A job worth 2,000 that leaves 600 after materials and labour is worth 600 to the business, and that is the most you could ever spend to win it and still break even.

Then allow for the fact that not every enquiry becomes a customer. If roughly one in four enquiries turns into a booked job, each enquiry is worth a quarter of that 600, so 150.

That 150 is your ceiling for cost per enquiry. Spending up to it breaks even on the first job. Spending well under it is profit. If customers come back or refer others, the real ceiling is higher, but start from the first sale and treat repeat business as a bonus rather than a justification.

For online shops the same logic produces a break-even return on ad spend instead of a cost per lead. The [break-even ROAS calculator](/tools/break-even-roas-calculator/) does that maths with product cost, delivery, fees and returns included.

## Step 2: estimate what a click costs

Google's Keyword Planner shows a range of top-of-page bids for each keyword. Treat it as a rough guide rather than a price list, because what you actually pay depends on your Quality Score, your competitors and how tightly the campaign is built.

If you have run ads before, your own average cost per click for the same keywords beats any estimate.

## Step 3: estimate your conversion rate

This is the number people guess most optimistically. Use your own history if you have it. If you do not, pick a cautious figure and expect to replace it with a real one after the first month. Published industry averages mix very different businesses and very different definitions of a conversion, so they are a poor basis for a budget.

## Step 4: do the maths

Say you want 20 enquiries a month, your conversion rate is 5%, and clicks cost around 3.

- Clicks needed: 20 &divide; 0.05 = 400 clicks
- Budget: 400 &times; 3 = 1,200 a month
- Cost per enquiry: 1,200 &divide; 20 = 60

Against a ceiling of 150, a cost per enquiry of 60 leaves plenty of room. If the same maths had produced 200, Google Ads would lose money at those numbers, and the honest move is to fix the conversion rate or the offer before spending anything.

The "Plan a month" section of the [Google Ads metrics calculator](/tools/metrics-calculator/) runs this calculation for you and gives the daily budget to set.

## Is the budget big enough to learn from?

A budget can be affordable and still too small to be useful.

If it buys a handful of clicks a day, a single good or bad day swings every number, and you cannot tell a working campaign from a lucky one. Automated bidding strategies need a steady flow of conversions to learn from. With only a few conversions a month, a strategy like target CPA is a setting rather than a strategy, and a simpler approach under close watch is usually more honest.

Two ways to make a small budget work harder:

1. **Concentrate it.** One campaign on your best service in your best area learns faster than five campaigns each starved of data
2. **Narrow the reach.** Fewer keywords, a tighter area, or ads only during the hours you can answer the phone

## How Google spends a daily budget

You set an average daily budget, not a hard daily cap. On busy days Google can spend up to twice that amount, and on quiet days less. Across a month it will not charge more than the daily budget multiplied by the average number of days in a month, 30.4.

So a daily budget of 40 means up to about 1,216 a month, with some days at 80 and others well below 40. That is normal behaviour, not a fault.

## When "Limited by budget" appears

The status means the campaign could have shown on more searches if it had more money. That is not automatically a reason to raise the budget.

If cost per conversion is comfortably below your ceiling, raising the budget is usually the right call. If it is not, narrow the campaign first. More budget on a campaign that is losing money just loses money faster.

## When to increase the budget

Increase it when two things are true at once: cost per conversion is below your target, and the campaign is losing impression share because of budget. Raise it in steps rather than doubling overnight. Google says a budget change does not put automated bidding back into learning, but a much larger budget buys more marginal clicks, and cost per conversion often rises before you can see whether the extra spend is worth it.

Ad spend is what Google charges for clicks. A management fee is what you pay whoever runs the account. Keep them apart when you plan.
