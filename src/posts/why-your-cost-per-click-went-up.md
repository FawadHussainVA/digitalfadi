Cost per click climbs, the budget buys fewer visits, and the obvious reaction is to lower bids. That usually makes things worse, because cost per click is a symptom rather than a cause, and lowering bids treats the symptom.

Here is what sits behind it, and which parts you can actually change.

## Your Quality Score dropped

Google ranks adverts on a combination of bid, expected click-through rate, advert relevance and landing page experience. A lower Quality Score means you pay more for the same position, or lose it.

Quality Score is not a vanity metric. It is the lever that lets a smaller advertiser outrank a bigger one while paying less, and it moves when relevance moves.

Common causes: adverts rewritten to be more general, keywords added to an ad group that no longer match its adverts, or a landing page redesign that removed the text matching the search.

## Match types widened

Somebody switched phrase match to broad, or added broad match keywords without the negatives to contain them.

Broad match reaches searches with looser intent, and looser intent carries lower click-through rates, which pulls Quality Score down, which raises cost per click. The account often looks like it is reaching more people while paying more for each one.

## Competitors entered the auction

You do not control this and it is frequently the whole answer. A new competitor, an existing one raising budgets, or a seasonal entrant changes the price of the same click.

The auction insights report shows it. If impression share dropped while overlap rate rose, somebody new is bidding against you.

This is worth knowing precisely because it stops you from looking for a fault that is not there.

## Seasonality

Legal, insurance, travel, retail at Christmas, and anything tied to a sales cycle all have periods where everybody bids harder for the same inventory.

Compare against the same period last year rather than against last month. Month on month comparisons in a seasonal market generate false alarms constantly.

## Bidding strategy is chasing a target it cannot hit

A target cost per acquisition set lower than the account can achieve will often raise cost per click rather than lower it, because the algorithm concentrates spend on the few auctions it believes will convert, and those are the expensive ones.

The same happens with target return on ad spend set above what the account has ever achieved.

Strategies need conversion data. A target strategy on a handful of conversions a month is a setting, not a strategy.

## Performance Max is taking the cheap traffic

If Performance Max runs alongside Search without brand exclusions, it absorbs the cheapest, highest intent traffic, including people searching your own name.

Search is left with the more expensive non-brand auctions, so its cost per click rises. The account total may be unchanged, but the Search campaign looks like it has deteriorated and often gets cut.

More on separating them in [Performance Max: what it hides and how to see it](/learn/performance-max-what-it-hides/).

## The landing page changed

Landing page experience feeds Quality Score. A redesign that made the page slower, removed the copy matching the search, or buried the form will raise what you pay for every click afterwards.

Website changes are rarely communicated to whoever runs the advertising. It is worth asking what changed on the site in the weeks before the increase.

## What to do instead of cutting bids

Lowering bids reduces position, which reduces click-through rate, which reduces Quality Score, which raises cost per click. It is a loop that feels like control and is not.

Work through it in this order:

1. Check auction insights. If competitors entered, the price changed and nothing is broken
2. Compare year on year, not month on month
3. Look at Quality Score components and find which one dropped
4. Read the search terms report and tighten match types with negatives
5. Check whether Performance Max is taking brand traffic from Search
6. Ask what changed on the website

## The number that actually matters

Cost per click is not a business metric. You can halve it by bidding on worthless traffic.

What matters is cost per lead or cost per sale. If cost per click rose and cost per lead held, nothing bad happened. If cost per click held and cost per lead rose, the problem is conversion rather than auction.

A [Google Ads audit](/google-ads-audit/) separates the two and tells you which one you have. If the account is not producing enquiries at all, start with [why your Google Ads are not converting](/learn/why-are-my-google-ads-not-converting/).
