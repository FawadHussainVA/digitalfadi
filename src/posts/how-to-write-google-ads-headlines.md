Google shows up to three of your headlines at a time, picked from as many as fifteen, in whatever combination it predicts will perform best for that search. The headlines that get shown most are the ones that are clearly different from each other, relevant to what was searched, and readable next to any other headline in any order.

Write fifteen versions of the same sentence and Google has nothing to choose between. Write fifteen headlines that each do a different job and it has real options.

## How a responsive search ad works

- Up to **15 headlines**, each up to **30 characters**
- Up to **4 descriptions**, each up to **90 characters**
- At least **3 headlines** and **2 descriptions** are required
- Google shows up to **3 headlines** and **2 descriptions** at once, and not always all of them
- Two **display paths** of up to **15 characters** each, shown after your domain

Because any headline can appear beside any other, in any position, each one has to make sense on its own. A headline that only works as the second half of a sentence will sometimes appear first, or alone.

## Give every headline a job

The easiest way to get real variety is to decide what each headline is for before writing it.

1. **Relevance (2 to 3 headlines).** Include the main search term, because people scan for the words they typed and relevance feeds Quality Score. "Boiler Repair in Leeds"
2. **Outcome (2 to 3).** What the customer gets, not what you are. "Heating Back On Same Day"
3. **Proof (2 to 3).** Specific, true facts: years trading, accreditations, review ratings you can show. Only use numbers you can back up
4. **Offer or price (1 to 2).** Fixed prices, free quotes, a guarantee. "Fixed Price, No Call-Out Fee"
5. **Differentiator (1 to 2).** Why you over the four other ads on the page: speed, area, specialism
6. **Call to action (1 to 2).** "Book Online in 2 Minutes"
7. **Brand (1).** Your business name, for people who already know it

That adds up to roughly a dozen distinct headlines, which is plenty. Quality beats filling every slot.

## Use the space, but not with filler

A 12-character headline wastes most of a line that a competitor will fill. Most headlines should use most of the 30 characters, but never pad them with words like "Quality" and "Professional" that every advertiser uses and nobody believes.

Specifics beat adjectives. "Fitted Within 48 Hours" says more than "Fast, Reliable Service" in the same space.

## Descriptions carry the proof

Descriptions get more room and less attention. Use them to support the headlines rather than repeat them: what is included, what makes you credible, and what to do next. Each description, like each headline, must read correctly on its own and alongside any other.

## Pinning: when and how

Pinning fixes a headline to a position, so it always shows there. It also removes every combination that would have placed other headlines in that position, which shrinks what Google can test.

Pin only when you must, for legal wording or a required brand line. When you do, pin two or three headlines to the same position, so Google still has a choice rather than one fixed line.

## Ad strength is a guide, not a goal

Ad strength rates how much variety and relevance your assets offer. Google states it is not used to decide which ads win the auction. It is a helpful nudge towards more distinct headlines, but chasing "Excellent" by stuffing in keyword variations produces ads that score well and read badly.

Judge ads on clicks and conversions, not on the rating.

## Check which headlines are working

After an ad has had enough impressions, Google labels individual assets by performance. Give new headlines time before judging them, because labels in the first weeks are based on too little data. Then replace the consistently weakest with a headline that does a different job, rather than a reworded version of the same one.

## Mistakes that keep headlines from showing

1. **Near-duplicates.** "Emergency Plumber Leeds" and "Leeds Emergency Plumbers" are one headline to Google
2. **Headlines that need a partner.** "And We Do It Fast" makes no sense on its own
3. **Over-pinning.** One pinned headline in each position leaves almost nothing to test
4. **Unsupported claims.** "Number 1 in Leeds" invites disapproval and distrust
5. **Over the limit.** Google Ads rejects headlines over 30 characters, usually at the moment you are trying to publish
