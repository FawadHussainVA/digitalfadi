Audits have a reputation problem. Too many are a template run against an account, producing forty observations of equal weight, most of which do not matter and none of which are ordered by cost.

A useful audit does two things a template cannot. It checks in an order where each step makes the next readable, and it tells you what each finding is costing.

## Why the order matters more than the checklist

Every finding in a Google Ads account is downstream of one question: is the conversion data real?

If conversions are double counted, bidding has been optimising towards inflated numbers for months. Every performance judgement you make afterwards is built on that. "This campaign has a good cost per acquisition" means nothing if the acquisitions are counted twice.

So an audit that reviews bidding strategy before verifying tracking has produced an opinion about a number it has not checked.

## 1. Conversion tracking, end to end

Not "is there a conversion action", but how each one fires and whether it fires once.

- Every conversion action listed, with what triggers it
- Whether a hard-coded tag and a GA4 import are both counting the same event
- Whether a thank-you page counts again on refresh
- Whether conversion values are passed, and whether they include tax and shipping consistently
- Whether GA4 and Google Ads report the same totals for the same day
- For lead generation, whether a form fill and a qualified enquiry are counted as the same thing

This single section finds more money than the rest of an audit combined.

## 2. Brand separated from non-brand

Brand searches convert cheaply and make any account look healthy. Most of those clicks were already yours through the organic listing.

If brand and non-brand sit in the same reporting, the account's average cost per acquisition is a blend of demand you created and demand you already had. The number is real and it answers no useful question.

An audit should report them apart, every time.

## 3. Search terms, as far back as the data goes

Not the top twenty. All of it, grouped.

The search terms report shows what you actually paid for rather than what you asked for. In most accounts there are whole categories nobody chose: job seekers, students, competitors researching, people looking for free versions, and searches for services you do not offer.

This is also where the negative keyword list gets judged. Most lists were built at launch and never revisited.

## 4. Account structure

Campaign and ad group architecture, match types, how budgets are shared, whether campaigns compete with each other.

The common faults are a budget spread so thin across campaign types that none collects enough conversions for bidding to learn from, and ad groups holding keywords so varied that no advert can be relevant to all of them.

## 5. Performance Max, specifically

It deserves its own section because the interface hides most of it.

- Are brand terms excluded, or is it rebuying demand you already had?
- Is there one asset group for everything, or one per product line?
- What does the channel breakdown show once it is extracted?
- Are audience signals built from your own customer data or guessed?

More on this in [Performance Max: what it hides and how to see it](/learn/performance-max-what-it-hides/).

## 6. Shopping and the product feed

For retail accounts the feed decides performance before bidding does. Titles, attributes, identifiers, disapprovals, availability accuracy. Feed problems look exactly like bidding problems and cannot be bid away.

## 7. Bidding

Only now. Strategy choice, target settings, and whether the account has the conversion volume the strategy needs. A target cost per acquisition strategy on four conversions a month is a setting, not a strategy.

## 8. Landing pages

Not a redesign. A check on whether the page answers the search that paid to arrive there, and whether the form asks for more than it needs.

## What the output should look like

A written document, in plain language, with findings ordered by what each is costing. Each one saying what the problem is, what it costs, and what you would do about it.

Not a sixty page export of screenshots. Not a scorecard out of a hundred. Not a list of forty observations with no weighting.

You should be able to hand it to another agency or fix things in house. If an audit is only usable by the agency that wrote it, that tells you what it was for.
