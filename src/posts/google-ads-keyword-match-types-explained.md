Google Ads has three keyword match types, and each decides how closely a search must relate to your keyword before your ad can show. Exact match is the narrowest: searches with the same meaning as the keyword. Phrase match is wider: searches that include the meaning of the keyword, with other words around it. Broad match is the widest: any search Google judges to be related, even without your words in it. Narrower means more control and fewer searches; broader means more reach and more waste to manage.

## How to write each match type

- **Broad match:** boiler repair (no symbols)
- **Phrase match:** "boiler repair" (in quotation marks)
- **Exact match:** [boiler repair] (in square brackets)

## Exact match: same meaning, not same words

Exact match used to mean the exact words. It now means the same meaning or intent. That includes plurals, misspellings, a different word order and close paraphrases where the meaning does not change.

For the keyword [boiler repair], the ad can show for:

- boiler repair
- boiler repairs
- repair boiler
- searches Google judges to mean the same thing, such as fix my boiler

It should not show for boiler repair course or new boiler cost, because the meaning changes.

## Phrase match: the meaning, plus other words

Phrase match shows your ad for searches that include the meaning of your keyword, with other words before or after it. Word order matters when changing it would change the meaning.

For the keyword "boiler repair", the ad can show for:

- emergency boiler repair
- boiler repair near me
- cheapest boiler repair in Leeds

That last example is the catch. Phrase match brings in the useful extra words, such as near me and emergency, and the unwanted ones, such as cheapest, course or jobs. [Negative keywords](/learn/how-to-find-negative-keywords/) are how you remove the unwanted ones.

## Broad match: anything Google judges related

Broad match can show your ad for searches that do not contain your keyword at all, if Google decides they are related. To judge that, it uses signals such as the other keywords in the ad group, your landing page and the searcher's recent searches.

For the keyword boiler repair, the ad can show for:

- boiler service
- heating engineer near me
- no hot water
- new boiler cost

Some of those are excellent, such as no hot water, which is a customer in trouble. Others are a different job or a researcher. Broad match finds searches you would never have thought to add, and spends money on searches you would never have chosen.

## Which match type should you use?

There is no single right answer, but there is a sensible default for each situation:

1. **Small budget or new account:** exact and phrase match. Every click is precious, so keep the searches close to what you sell
2. **Manual bidding:** exact and phrase. Broad match without automated bidding has nothing steering it away from poor searches
3. **Automated bidding with accurate, frequent conversions:** broad match becomes worth testing. Google designs broad match to work with Smart Bidding, which uses each search's signals to bid down on poor ones. [Which Google Ads bid strategy should you use?](/learn/which-google-ads-bid-strategy-should-you-use/) covers when automated bidding is ready
4. **Your most important keywords:** keep them in exact match as well, so you control their ads and see their results clearly

The common pattern in well-run accounts is exact and phrase match for the core, with broad match added carefully once conversion tracking is reliable and the search terms report is checked often.

## The condition broad match depends on

Broad match is only as good as the conversion data guiding it. If the account counts page views or button clicks as conversions, broad match plus automated bidding will find more of the searches that produce page views and button clicks. That is how accounts end up with rising spend, healthy-looking conversion numbers and no extra enquiries. [Why are my Google Ads not converting?](/learn/why-are-my-google-ads-not-converting/) explains how to check what is being counted.

## When the same keyword is in more than one match type

If a search is identical to one of your keywords, Google prefers that keyword, whatever its match type, over other keywords that could also match. That makes it practical to run a keyword in exact match for control alongside broader versions for discovery, without the two simply competing at random.

## Check the search terms report, whatever you choose

Match types decide which searches are possible. The search terms report shows which searches actually happened. In Google Ads it is under Insights and reports, then Search terms.

Read it weekly while a campaign is new. Add the wrong searches as negatives, and add the good ones you were not bidding on as keywords of their own. For large reports, the [search terms n-gram analyser](/tools/n-gram-analyser/) groups searches by shared words, so patterns of waste show up as one line instead of fifty.

## Common match type mistakes

1. **Broad match on a small budget with manual bids.** The budget goes on loosely related searches before it reaches the good ones
2. **Assuming exact match means exact words.** It matches close variants and same-meaning searches too
3. **Never reading the search terms report.** Every match type, including exact, can bring in searches you would not choose
4. **Switching everything to broad at once.** Test it in one campaign against the existing setup, and judge on enquiries or sales, not clicks
5. **Forgetting that negatives work differently.** Negative keywords do not match close variants, so plurals and misspellings must be added separately
